CremonaCad

CremonaCad is a free and open source application for designing violin family instruments!

Rather than a static trace, or a complex list of coordinates, CremonaCad defines instruments using a simple system of intersecting arcs. These patterns are historically informed by a drafting document found in the workshop of Enrico Ceruti, as well as the research of American luthiers David Beard and Kevin Kelly.

Upload a historical instrument and use it as a reference, or go off the rails and create something totally unique! :D

Once your design is complete, CremonaCad can create moulds and templates for your specific instrument. These patterns can be exported either as PDFs for traditional hand-making, or as SVGs for computer assisted fabrication.

Tutorial

CremonaCad uses input controls on the right side of the screen to draw your instrument on the canvas in real time. The process is defined by a series of sections which build upon one another, drawing each part of the instrument step by step. Once all the sections are complete, you can generate moulds and templates for construction.

Basic Controls

  • Zoom: Use the scroll wheel to zoom in and out toward your cursor. On a trackpad, use a pinch gesture to zoom. The + and - keys also zoom toward the canvas center.
  • Pan: Hold Space and drag with the left mouse button, or drag with the middle mouse button to pan. On a trackpad, use a two-finger scroll to pan. Arrow keys also pan the camera — hold Shift for larger steps.
  • Press F to fit the entire design back into view at any time.
  • The Axis controls on the bottom right of the canvas allow for custom grid settings.
  • Files can be saved and loaded using the + (New), ↓ (Save), and ↑ (Load) icon buttons at the top right of the screen.
  • CremonaCad comes with a set of predefined templates from historical instruments. Select one to study, or create a new design.

Drawing Tools

The toolbar down the left edge of the canvas is a small drafting set, separate from the recipe panels on the right. Nothing you draw with it feeds into the instrument — it is there to work things out alongside the recipe: sketch a proportion you are testing, trace a feature off a photograph, mark a measurement, or lay out an f-hole by hand. Your drawing is saved inside the design file along with everything else.

Drawing a shape

  • Every tool accepts two gestures: drag from one point to the next, or click once and click again. The second is easier on a trackpad, and it is the only way the arc tools work — they take three separate clicks rather than a drag.
  • Escape steps back one level at a time: it abandons a shape you are part way through, then returns you to the arrow (Select), then clears your selection.
  • Each tool has a letter — Line, Arc, Circle, Rectangle, Section, Text, Point, Offset, Image. Buttons carrying a small caret hold several related tools; press the letter again to step through them, or click the caret to see them all. The button keeps showing whichever you used last.
  • The Color swatch in the settings bar along the bottom sets the colour of the selected shape, or of the next one you draw if nothing is selected.

Snapping

  • As you draw, points pull onto the geometry already on screen — both the recipe's own arcs and anything you have drawn yourself. A small green glyph tells you what you caught: a square for an endpoint, a crosshair in a circle for an arc or circle centre, a diamond for a point somewhere along a path.
  • Snapping to a path also captures its direction, which is what lets the tangent arcs continue smoothly out of whatever you started on.

Arcs

Since the instrument is built from arcs, the arc button holds five ways of striking one. All five take clicks, not drags, and Shift asks for the other solution — the long way round instead of the short, or the opposite curve.

  • Arc — click the centre, then a point setting the radius and where the arc begins, then a third fixing where it ends.
  • Arc (start first) — the same three clicks with the first two swapped, for when you know where the arc must start but not yet where its centre lies.
  • Tangent Arc — click a point on an existing curve, then an end point. The radius is solved so the new arc leaves the old one smoothly, with no visible joint.
  • Chained Tangent Arc — the same idea, continued: after the first arc, every further click adds another arc running smoothly out of the last. Escape ends the chain. Each arc is still its own shape, so any one of them can be adjusted afterwards.
  • Join Arc — bridges two existing curves. Click an endpoint on each and it fits the arc, or pair of arcs, that meets both smoothly. If it picks the wrong one, tap Shift to cycle through the alternatives.

Offset, dimensions and sections

  • Offset works the other way round from the drawing tools: select first, then pick up the tool, then move the cursor to the side you want and click. The distance follows your cursor and is shown as you go — or simply type a number and press Enter for an exact one. Lines, arcs and circles can be offset; a run of shapes meeting end to end offsets together as one curve.
  • Dimension draws a measured line between two points and labels it — useful with snapping for reading a distance off the design.
  • Section divides a line into weighted parts, alternating between two colours, for showing a ratio. Set the parts in the settings bar as a list like 1,1,1 or 3,4,3.

Adjusting what you have drawn

  • With the arrow tool, click a shape to select it, Shift-click to add more, or drag a box across the canvas to catch several at once.
  • The settings bar along the bottom shows the selected shape's numbers — a line's two endpoints and its length, an arc's centre, radius, start and end angle — and every one of them can be typed into directly when eyeballing it is not good enough.
  • Drag a shape to move it, or drag one of its endpoints to reshape it. Arrow keys nudge the selection in small steps, Shift with them in larger ones. Delete removes it, and Ctrl+Z undoes.
  • Holding Shift while drawing a line locks it to common angles — 30°, 45°, 90° and so on. On a box it forces a square. The same key on the arc tools asks for the other arc.
  • The Compass toggle on a selected arc shows its centre and radius lines, so you can see how it was struck.

Layers

  • The Layers button opens your list of layers. New shapes land on whichever is active. Double-click a name to rename it, use its eye to hide it and its lock to protect it from stray clicks.
  • The small eye on the Layers button itself hides everything you have drawn at once — handy for comparing your sketch against the recipe underneath, or checking the recipe on its own.

Reference Images

  • The Reference Image button opens your list of reference images — photos or scans placed on the canvas to trace over. Upload Image at the foot of that panel adds one (or press I), and each image gets its own row with an eye and a lock. Place as many as you like — plan, long arch, cross arch — and park the ones you aren't tracing with their eye.
  • A freshly uploaded image is unlocked so you can position and scale it: drag it to move, drag a corner to scale proportionally or an edge to scale one dimension, drag the handle above it to rotate, arrow keys to nudge, Ctrl+Z to undo. Its name, exact position, size, rotation, opacity and white-removal toggle are in the settings bar along the bottom while it's selected.
  • Mirror in that same settings bar flips the image content left-right — for a scan that came out reversed, or to compare a traced half against its opposite. Combine with Rot° to mirror on any axis, not just vertical. Only the picture flips; its position, size and rotation are untouched.
  • Press Lock in that settings bar when you're done placing it. A locked image can't be selected or dragged by accident, so clicks pass straight through it to whatever you're drawing on top. Images loaded from a template or a saved file start locked for the same reason. Click an image's name in the panel to unlock and select it again.
  • The small eye on the Reference Image button hides or shows every image at once.

Base Measurements

This section sets the primary dimensions of the instrument, as well as basic file properties.

  • Use the primary dimensions section to define the height and width of your instrument. If you are tracing a reference image, find the real dimensions of that instrument first and enter them here — a newly placed reference image is fitted to these bounds, and you can then type an exact width or height into the settings bar to scale it precisely.
  • For many measurements, ratios will be displayed to the right of the metric value. Many Cremonese instruments use simple integer ratios which define their arc segments.
  • Define rib and overhang values to create a total inset value. This inset will bound the inner path of the instrument.

Main Bouts

The main bouts define the upper and lower sections of the instrument's body. These will be the first arc segments you create.

  • The join arcs U0 and L0 (Upper and Lower) are used to connect the Vesica arcs U1 and L1.
  • Each bout has a width measurement which sets the total outer width of the instrument including the inset. These width values are often provided in online measurements of historical instruments, and are helpful in guiding your design.
  • Use the Viol Neck option to add a viola da gamba-style upper extension. These are of course common on double bass.

Corners

This section defines both the position of the corners and the arc segments that connect them to the main bouts.

  • XY controls exist for each corner placement. In general I have tried to avoid cartesian coordinates, but this allows for precise adjustments.
  • The Use Viol Corner option allows for the creation of viol-style corners, which only have one connecting arc.

Center Bout

The center bout is the narrowest section of the instrument, connecting the upper and lower bouts.

  • The Center Bout Width defines the total outer width at the narrowest point. When adjusting the C0 circle, this width will remain constant.
  • The Fit C0 to Bouts option automatically calculates C0 to cleanly intersect U2 and L2. This is based off of Kevin Kelly's four-circle method, while still allowing for manual adjustments.

Outer Path

The outer path section defines the corner arcs and button that complete the instrument outline.

  • Each corner is defined by at least two arcs and respective cutoff angles. The cutoff angle trims the arc at a point, determining the final length of the corner tip.
  • The button is the small semicircular tab at the neck joint on the back plate. You can define its radius here. It does not appear on the top.

Purfling & Fluting

These sections define the plate-edge details inset from the outer outline.

  • Purfling is a narrow inlaid strip set just inside the plate edge. The offset controls how far from the edge the channel begins; the width sets the channel's span.
  • Fluting is the shallow cove carved between the purfling and the rising arch. Offset and Reach are both measured from the plate edge — Offset to the platform's outer boundary, Reach to its inner boundary, where the arch takes off.

Long Arching

This section shapes the lengthwise profile of the top and back plates — how the arch rises from end to end along the centerline.

  • Choose a Catenary or Cycloid curve for a smooth, symmetric arch, or a Spline to place your own control points along the profile.
  • Arch Height sets the peak height above the plate edge; Thickness sets the plate thickness at that edge.
  • Each spline point carries a Mirror toggle, which repeats it at the opposite end of the plate. Turn it off and that point stands alone, so the profile no longer has to be symmetric about its peak — the upper and lower halves can be shaped independently, the way they usually are on a real instrument.
  • Arch Depth lowers where the arch takes off from the plate edge, giving the fluting channel more room to curve into.

Cross Arching

This section shapes the arch's cross-section — how it rises across the width of the plate at any point along its length. The center peak follows the long arch you already set.

  • Drag the Cross Section Height slider to preview the profile at different points along the body.
  • Cycloid Factor and Percent shape the curve's character and how steeply it takes off from the edge. Choose a Spline instead to place the cross-section's control points by hand.
  • Turn on Asymmetric (left / right) to give each side of the plate its own factor and percentage, so one flank can be fuller than the other.
  • A single cross-section repeated the whole length of the plate is a simplification. Press + Set Station at … to pin the current cross-section at the height you are previewing, then move the slider and set another. Each station holds its own shape, and the surface flows smoothly between them — which is how you get a rounder centre bout and flatter ends, or any other change in character along the body.
  • Your stations are listed in a table above the controls. Click a station's height to jump the preview back to it; while you are parked on one, the fields below edit that station rather than the plate as a whole.
  • Toggle Flat Platform to preview the pre-carving state, before the fluting channel is gouged.
  • Enable Wireframe or Contour Map to inspect the full interpolated surface in 3D.

Mould

The mould section defines the internal blocks and templates used to construct the instrument. These blocks form the wooden structure around which the ribs are bent and glued.

  • Four blocks are defined: Upper, Upper Corner, Lower Corner, and Lower. Each block has height and width dimensions.
  • Corner block Pad values add extra material at each corner point. This can help with finding the optimal placing of the block against the mould.
  • If the mould is to be cut on a CNC, the Bit Diameter value prevent issues with right angles at critical glue points.
  • The default mould has clamping channels parallel to the edges. However, a simple box cutout can be specified as well.

Export

Once your design is complete, you can export templates and patterns for construction. Traces, the mould, and block templates are available as SVG, PDF, and DXF; the arched top and back plates export as STL for CNC carving.

Andrew Argraves

A musician and software engineer based out of New Haven, CT.

CremonaCad started as a personal tool to help me understand the geometry behind historical violin patterns. After months of development and experimentation, I realized that it might be genuinely useful in developing the craft of violin-making.

I plan to keep developing the application with features like arching templates, f-hole patterns, and neck profiles. As of Spring 2026, Cremona Cad is still early in its development lifecycle, I hope to use it as an actual tool in the making of my first instruments.

CremonaCad is open source, and I welcome feedback, ideas, and collaboration from anyone in the making community.
